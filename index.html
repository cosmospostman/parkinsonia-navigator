Continent-scale woody weed mapping

Weeds follow water. We follow weeds. Everywhere.

Woody weeds like prickly acacia, rubber vine and parkinsonia have their own subtle rhythm of green-up and dry-off, distinct from native vegetation. We read every Sentinel-1 and -2 satellite pass of the year to map them, stand by stand, across northern Australia.

Target species

Three Weeds of National Significance

  • Parkinsonia

    Shrub

    Parkinsonia aculeata

    A thorny shrub whose near-impenetrable thickets wall off creeks, bore drains and wetlands, and cut stock off from water. Mustering through it is slow and costly, and it keeps spreading along drainage lines.

    Seasonal habits Sheds its tiny leaflets in drought but photosynthesises through its green stems, so it stays partly green after the grass around it has cured.

    Weak to
    • Slow, hot grass fires, above all on seedlings
    • Basal bark spray on stems up to 15 cm
    • A seed beetle, Penthobruchus germaini
    Resists
    • Floods: the water carries its pods to new country
    • Drought: its hard-coated seed waits for good seasons
    • Clearing: its seed sprouts afterwards

  • Rubber vine

    Vine

    Cryptostegia grandiflora

    A poisonous vine that smothers riverbank trees to the top of the canopy. Its thickets hide cattle, which makes a muster far more costly, and leave almost no pasture.

    Seasonal habits Away from permanent water it drops its leaves as the soil dries and reshoots after even a little rain. In our 2025 measurements near Karumba, its greenness fell by more than two thirds while its radar echo barely moved.

    Weak to
    • Fire two years running
    • Foliar spray on plants under 2 m
    • A rust fungus and a leaf moth, which cut seeding
    Resists
    • Foliar spray, once stems pass 8 cm
    • Biocontrol: it slows seeding, never kills
    • Containment: up to 450 seeds a pod, spread by wind and water

  • Prickly acacia

    Tree

    Vachellia nilotica

    A thorny tree that turns open Mitchell grass downs into thorn woodland. It crowds out the grass, hinders mustering and blocks stock from water.

    Seasonal habits Loses most of its leaves between June and November. Even at medium density it halves the grass around it, so less grass greens up in the wet season, and its woody frame returns radar all year.

    Weak to
    • Grubbing, ploughing or chain pulling, May to September
    • Basal bark, cut stump or soil-applied herbicide
    • A seed beetle that takes up to 80% of seed in places
    Resists
    • Fire, once past the seedling stage
    • Grazing: cattle eat its pods and spread the seed
    • Pushing: seedlings flood up near water after it

01 · Plant biology from space

Radar, sunlight and a year in 64 numbers

How Sentinel-1 radar reads a plant

Sentinel-1 carries a radar. It sends pulses of microwaves at the ground and records the echoes. Microwaves pass through cloud, and the radar lights its own scene, so it works through the wet season and at night.

What a 5.5 cm wave can see

The pulse has a wavelength of about 5.5 cm, called C-band. A wave responds to things near its own size, and a canopy is full of them: leaves and medium-sized branches scatter the pulse. So C-band reaches only the top layers of a dense canopy.

Much smaller things do not disturb the pulse. It passes through cloud droplets, and only heavy rain shows in the image. (A weather radar is built to catch the faint echo of rain. The Bureau of Meteorology's radar on Mornington Island uses the same 5 cm band as Sentinel-1, and its Mount Isa radar uses the 10 cm band.)

Anything much larger than the wave, such as a trunk, a rock face or the ground, acts as a surface. A smooth surface is a mirror. It sends the whole pulse off in one direction, and unless that direction is straight back at the radar, the surface shows black in the image. A rough surface scatters the pulse in all directions, so part of it always returns. To Sentinel-1, bumps more than about 2 mm high start to look rough. So still water is black, and wind ripples and ploughed soil show up.

Moisture counts as well. Water reflects microwaves strongly and dry material reflects them weakly, so sappy plants and damp soil are bright, and cured grass on dry ground is dim.

A sideways look, and two receivers

A radar tells one piece of ground from the next by the time each echo takes to return. If it looked straight down, ground the same distance to the left and to the right would answer at the same moment. So Sentinel-1 looks to one side, 29 to 46 degrees from vertical, and the echoes arrive in order from the near edge of its 250 km swath to the far edge.

The slant shapes the image. Flat ground never points back at a radar that looks from the side. Only something rough or upright turns part of the pulse back, so the brightness of a pixel measures the roughness and structure of what stands there.

The radar also measures how the trip changed the pulse. A wave vibrates in one plane, and the direction of that plane is its polarisation. Horizontal (H) is level with the ground, and vertical (V) is at right angles to it. Over land, Sentinel-1 sends a vertical wave and listens on two receivers: one for the part that returns still vertical (VV), and one for the part that returns turned to horizontal (VH).

  • VV: how much came back. A wave that bounces once keeps its polarisation, so VV measures the plain reflection. Rough, damp surfaces return a lot, and smooth surfaces return little.
  • VH: did it come back from inside something? Inside a canopy the wave bounces many times, off twigs and leaves that point in every direction, so part of the echo returns turned to horizontal. A bare surface does little of this.

That is a simplification. A canopy raises VV as well as VH, so the two together say more than either one.

In our 2025 measurements a rubber vine thicket returned much the same echo in leaf and leafless, and native timber returned much the same as the thicket. So radar finds woody plants, but it cannot tell a weed from native timber.

Power, and a dawn and dusk orbit

From 693 km up, the radar transmits at a peak of about 4.4 kW, the draw of two 2.2 kW kitchen kettles. It holds that power only in bursts: each pulse lasts less than a ten-thousandth of a second, and the radar transmits for at most 12% of the time. The solar panels supply an average of 4.8 kW at the end of the satellite's design life, less than a 7 kW home EV charger draws. That runs the radar, the computers, the heaters and the data link to Earth.

Very little of that power returns. ESA does not publish an echo power. Our rough estimate, from the standard radar equation, is that each echo carries around a hundred-trillionth of the power sent. The instrument measures small differences in a signal that faint.

A radar needs plenty of electrical power and no daylight, and the orbit suits both. Sentinel-1 crosses the equator at 6 pm local time heading north and near 6 am heading south, along the line between day and night. Canada's RADARSAT-1 flew the same kind of orbit, and its published reason is that a dawn-dusk track keeps the solar panels in near-continuous sunlight.

How Sentinel-2 reads a plant by sunlight

Sentinel-2 carries a camera. It sends nothing. It collects sunlight reflected from the ground, in thirteen bands from 443 to 2190 nanometres. The longest of those waves is about two thousandths of a millimetre, and the radar's wave is 25,000 times longer. A wave of light is smaller than a leaf cell, so it responds to what a leaf is made of: pigment, cell structure and water. Radar reads the frame of a plant, and sunlight reads its chemistry.

Red and near-infrared: pigment and leaf cells

Chlorophyll, the pigment in leaves, absorbs visible light to power photosynthesis. It absorbs most in the blue and the red and least in the green, so plants look green. A canopy in full growth is dark in the red band, and it brightens as the leaves yellow, cure or fall.

Plants do not use near-infrared for photosynthesis. The cells of a leaf reflect it strongly, and the air spaces between them scatter it. So a healthy leaf is dark in red and bright in near-infrared, and little else in the landscape shows that pair. The standard greenness score, NDVI, is the contrast between the two bands: near zero where there are no green leaves, and 0.8 to 0.9 over the densest canopy. On this page, “greenness” means NDVI.

Between 700 and 800 nm a leaf's reflectance climbs steeply, from dark red to bright near-infrared. That slope is the red edge. Sentinel-2 has three bands on that slope, and a 2011 study showed that they improve estimates of how much chlorophyll a canopy holds.

Short-wave infrared: water in the leaf

The two longest bands respond to moisture. Water in a green leaf absorbs short-wave infrared strongly, so a wetter leaf is darker in these bands, and it brightens as the plant dries. ESA lists vegetation moisture stress among the uses of these bands, and mappers also use them to find burnt country.

Their limit is dry material. To tell cured grass and leaf litter from bare soil, a sensor needs fine detail between 2000 and 2500 nm, and Sentinel-2 has a single band there.

The northern year, and a plant out of step

The tropical north has two seasons: the wet from October to April, and the dry from May to September. Its savannas are evergreen eucalypts over annual and perennial tropical grasses. The grasses grow fast in the wet, set seed around March, then die back and cure into fuel. The eucalypts hold their leaves and thin only a little as the soil dries. From orbit the whole country turns green and then straw-coloured each year, and most of that change is grass.

A woody weed keeps its own calendar inside that cycle. It can hold some green after the grass has cured, or drop its leaves while the native timber keeps them. Either way its pixel falls out of step with its neighbours, so we look for that timing more than for any single image.

A mid-morning orbit, and cloud

The camera needs the sun, so Sentinel-2 crosses the equator at 10:30 am local time, 786 km up. ESA chose that hour as a compromise between enough sunlight and little cloud. It passes every five days at the equator, but a pass under cloud records no ground, and at any moment cloud covers about two thirds of the Earth's surface.

In the north the cloud comes with the wet, when plants grow fastest. Mappers in the Gulf of Carpentaria left maps incomplete because they could use only cloud-free images, and mappers in Arnhem Land had trouble getting clear imagery early and late in the season.

Every Sentinel-2 image flags each pixel for cloud and cloud shadow, so we can set those pixels aside. The record that remains has holes, and the holes fall in the wet. So we use both sensors: sunlight gives chemistry when the sky is clear, and radar gives structure on every pass.

AlphaEarth: a year of country in 64 numbers

Our third source is a summary of many satellites. Google and Google DeepMind publish it as AlphaEarth Foundations, in a form called a vector embedding: a list of numbers that works like a grid reference on a map of likeness. Similar things sit close together on that map, and the distance between two things measures how alike they are.

What goes into it

AlphaEarth gives every 10 m square of land and shallow water 64 numbers, one for each dimension of the embedding, for each year from 2017 to 2024. The numbers describe how that square and its surrounds behaved over the year. The model reads the year's images from Sentinel-2, Sentinel-1 and Landsat 8 and 9. In training it also had to predict other records of the same ground, so the summary holds some of what they know:

  • ALOS PALSAR, a longer-wave radar
  • GEDI, a laser on the space station that measures canopy height
  • the Copernicus 30 m elevation model
  • ERA5-Land climate records
  • GRACE satellite gravity measurements
  • United States land cover maps
  • text from geolocated Wikipedia articles and species sightings recorded in GBIF

The model trained on more than three billion image frames. DeepMind describes that as petabytes of Earth observation data.

What it adds

Context. Our own time series describes one 10 m pixel. The embedding describes that pixel and its surrounds across the whole year, and it generally overcomes gaps from cloud and missing data.

Comparison. Comparing two places takes one small sum: multiply their 64 numbers pair by pair and add the results. A result near 1 means the two places are much alike, and a lower result means less alike. The map runs that sum between one pixel and every other, on the 2024 embeddings. The same sum finds every place that resembles a patch of known weed. The embeddings are also designed for sorting country into classes, and for finding change between one year and the next. They work from few examples, tens to hundreds of labelled points.

What it leaves out

The 64 numbers mean nothing one at a time. Only the whole set can be compared. And there is one embedding per year, so it cannot show when in the year a plant greened or dropped its leaves. Our own Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 series supply that timing.

02 · Satellite coverage

All your paddocks, scanned twice a week

Europe's fleet of Sentinel satellites images northern Australia every few days in a “lawnmower” pattern. Each satellite circles from pole to pole while the Earth turns underneath, so every lap crosses a fresh strip of country. The orbits keep step with the sun, so a satellite passes over any one place at the same time of day, every time: mid-morning for the optical satellites, and dawn or dusk for the radar.

Each pass records one strip, and each satellite works through the same pattern of strips on a fixed cycle. Two satellites of each type fly half an orbit apart, so the same spot is imaged twice as often. Neighbouring strips overlap, so some country is seen more often than the timetable promises.

Satellite visits this frequent let us notice subtle seasonal changes, such as vegetation green-up and dry-off. In 2025, a typical 10 km block of the Mitchell catchment was photographed on 88 days and scanned by radar about 30 times. Every radar pass in our 2025 record came from one satellite, Sentinel-1A, which retraces each strip every 12 days.

The entire Sentinel imagery collection, dating back to the first launch in 2014, is freely available to everybody. Since it began, the European Union has committed more than €13 billion to Copernicus, the programme the satellites belong to.

03 · Our software model

Every pass of the year, stacked into a record for every 10 m pixel

Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 observations are pictures. We take the entire year's worth of pictures, cut them into individual pixels (each 10 m across), and sort them by date to make an annual time series of all observations at each point. Here is a patch 960 m across on the Flinders River plain, with its 112 Sentinel-2 dates and 30 Sentinel-1 dates of 2025.

In the Sentinel-2 collection, we discard observations with cloud or shadow. Radar is not impeded by cloud, so it reads on every pass. What is left, in date order, is each pixel's record of its year. For illustration, the charts below draw two parts of that record: how green the pixel was, and how strong a radar echo it sent back.

In this scene, we have marked some sample pixels: six with parkinsonia and six without. As the country dries, the radar echo fades almost everywhere, but the parkinsonia pixels stay a little brighter than the weed-free ones. The likely reason is its stems and branches, which go on sending radar back after the grass has cured. No single observation is enough to tell the story. We need to see the whole year unfold.

We feed each pixel's whole year into a Temporal Attention Model. “Temporal” because it takes the readings in date order. “Attention” because it learns for itself which times of the year matter, and how changes such as drying relate to one another across the year. Alongside that record it reads the pixel's AlphaEarth embedding for the year. We taught ours with pixels where a weed was confirmed, and pixels where it was confirmed absent. For every pixel it returns a weed probability score from 0 to 100 for each of the three weeds. Painted back onto the ground they came from, the scores are the map.

04 · Catchment maps

Know where to look before the helicopter leaves the ground

Every weed, everywhere, all at once

The Flinders River catchment covers 109,716 km², or about 1.1 billion 10 m pixels. Flying all of it by helicopter, on survey lines 500 m apart, might cost $4 million, burn 200,000 litres of fuel, and take nearly two years of flying. Our weed distribution maps highlight infestations as small as a couple of trees, across the entire catchment. Land managers can survey priority areas first, or add them to flights already planned, such as mustering. And in the bigger picture, a catchment-wide view helps coordinate a catchment-wide response.
